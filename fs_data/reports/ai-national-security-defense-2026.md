# 2026: AI in National Security and Defense

**Date:** May 16, 2026  
**Topic:** Artificial Intelligence in National Security and Defense

---

## Executive Summary

Artificial intelligence has become a foundational component of modern warfare and defense strategy. As of mid-2026, the US Department of Defense runs more than 600 AI initiatives under its Chief Digital and AI Office (CDAO), spanning autonomous systems, battlefield data fusion, and administrative modernization. China, Russia, and NATO are investing heavily in "intelligentized warfare" capabilities, with AI-driven drones, targeting systems, and electronic warfare tools being tested in active conflicts in Ukraine and Gaza.

Policy frameworks are struggling to keep pace. While over 70 nations now support binding standards for meaningful human control over lethal autonomous weapons, the United States and other major powers continue to push for flexibility in application. Export controls on advanced semiconductors remain a key lever in great-power competition, and ethical questions around algorithmic targeting, civilian harm, and accountability persist. The defense-industrial base is also transforming: venture-backed startups like Anduril and Palantir are challenging traditional prime contractors, reshaping how the Pentagon procures and deploys AI. This report examines the state of AI in national security and defense in 2026 across military applications, major-power strategies, governance, ethics, and the innovation ecosystem.

---

## Key Findings

1. **The US now operates over 600 active AI projects under CDAO**, with flagship programs including the Replicator initiative (fielding thousands of attritable autonomous systems across domains), CJADC2 (AI-driven all-domain command and control), and AI-powered "wingman" digital assistants for commanders.

2. **AI is battle-tested in active conflicts.** Ukraine uses AI for drone targeting, counter-drone detection, and electronic warfare. Israel's "The Gospel" and "Fire Factory" AI targeting systems are operational in Gaza. Russia employs AI for target recognition in Iranian-sourced drones and for electronic warfare in Ukraine.

3. **China's PLA is pursuing "intelligentized warfare"** — integrating AI across drone swarms, electronic warfare, and command decision-making—while Russia continues to field AI-enabled capabilities despite sanctions and supply-chain constraints.

4. **Over 70 states now support a binding UN standard on meaningful human control** for lethal autonomous weapons systems, though the US position emphasizes "context-appropriate human judgment" over fixed thresholds. A December 2024 UN General Assembly resolution (166-3-15) hinted at a potential two-tiered approach: prohibiting some systems while regulating others.

5. **US export controls on advanced AI chips** (NVIDIA H100/H200/B200 and analogues)—updated in October 2022, October 2023, and 2024—restrict access to China and Russia, directly impacting both military AI development and commercial supply chains.

6. **The defense-tech venture ecosystem is booming.** Anduril (valued at ~$14 billion), Palantir ($480 million Maven Smart System contract), and a consortium including SpaceX, OpenAI, and Scale AI are reshaping Pentagon procurement. Venture capital is flowing into autonomous ships (Saronic), ocean surveillance (Saildrone), drones (Red Cat), and autonomous pilots (Shield AI).

7. **Legacy prime contractors are adapting through partnerships and acquisitions.** Lockheed Martin, RTX, and L3Harris are collaborating with startups and acquiring AI software capabilities, while the Pentagon plans to use AI for financial audits by end of 2026.

## Analysis

### Military AI Applications & Battlefield Integration

The battlefield integration of AI has accelerated dramatically since 2022. The US Department of Defense's Replicator initiative represents a doctrinal shift toward massed, attritable autonomous systems—thousands of low-cost drones and uncrewed vehicles designed to overwhelm adversary defenses across air, sea, and land. Replicator 2.0, explicitly framed as a counter to Chinese drone swarms, underscores the pacing threat of PLA capabilities. CJADC2 (Combined Joint All-Domain Command and Control) seeks to solve the long-standing military problem of data fusion: linking sensors from satellites, aircraft, ships, and ground units into a single AI-enhanced picture that enables faster, better-informed decisions.

In Ukraine, the war has become a live laboratory for AI in conventional conflict. Both Ukrainian and Russian forces employ AI for drone targeting, electronic warfare, and counter-drone detection. Russia's use of AI-enabled target recognition in Iranian-supplied drones demonstrates how even sanctioned states can integrate commercially available AI into weapons systems. Israel's use of "The Gospel" and "Fire Factory" in Gaza—AI systems that generate targeting recommendations and coordinate fire missions—illustrates a future in which AI plays a direct role in lethal decision-making at operational tempo.

China's "intelligentized warfare" concept goes beyond tactical applications, envisioning AI woven into command decision-making, logistics, and strategic planning. The PLA's emphasis on drone swarms and electronic warfare suggests a doctrine that seeks to paralyze an adversary's decision-making cycle through speed and volume that only AI can manage.

### Major Power Strategies (US, China, Russia, NATO)

The United States maintains a technological lead in AI for defense, but faces structural challenges in procurement speed and data integration. CDAO's role as a central AI authority is still maturing, and the Pentagon's traditional acquisition system struggles to match the iteration cycles of commercial AI. The Replicator program and the growing influence of venture-backed defense startups represent attempts to inject speed and innovation into the system.

China's strategy under Xi Jinping's "military-civil fusion" policy continues to blur the line between commercial AI advances and PLA capabilities. Despite US export controls on advanced semiconductors, China is investing heavily in domestic AI chip production and alternative architectures. The PLA's timetable for achieving AI-enabled "intelligentized" forces by 2027–2035 remains ambitious but is being pursued with state-directed urgency.

Russia's defense AI efforts have been constrained by sanctions and a weakened high-tech sector, but the war in Ukraine has provided real-world testing and adaptation. Russia's use of AI in electronic warfare and drone targeting, even with Iranian-sourced platforms, demonstrates an ability to field functional AI-enabled systems under severe constraints.

NATO updated its AI strategy in 2025, launched a €1 billion Innovation Fund, and is encouraging member states—including the UK, France, and Estonia—to develop national AI defense roadmaps. The alliance faces the challenge of integrating AI capabilities across 32 member states with varying technological maturity and legal frameworks.

### Policy, Governance & International Law

The governance of AI in national security remains fractured. The UN Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons' Group of Governmental Experts (GGE) on LAWS has become the primary multilateral forum, but progress is slow. The March 2026 Geneva session saw over 70 states support a binding standard for "meaningful human control" throughout a weapon's lifecycle, yet major disagreements persist on what that standard requires in practice.

The United States position—"context-appropriate human judgment"—deliberately avoids fixed definitions, preserving flexibility for emerging AI applications that may require different levels of human involvement. This stance puts the US at odds with the growing majority of states seeking a treaty with specific prohibitions.

The December 2024 UN General Assembly resolution (passed 166-3-15) hints at a possible compromise: a two-tiered approach that would prohibit certain AI weapons systems outright while regulating others through transparency and reporting requirements. Whether this framework can be translated into binding international law remains an open question.

The EU AI Act's military exemption means European defense AI development proceeds largely outside the strict regulatory framework applied to commercial AI. However, debate continues on export controls for AI-enabled defense technologies, particularly regarding sales to non-democratic states.

### Ethical & Legal Challenges

Four interconnected ethical and legal challenges dominate the discourse. First, **algorithmic bias in targeting** — AI systems trained on historical data can replicate or amplify human biases, potentially leading to disproportionate or discriminative use of force. Second, **civilian harm risks** from autonomous strikes are heightened when AI systems operate at machine speeds, making human review difficult or impossible in practice. Third, **accountability gaps** arise when AI makes or substantially influences lethal decisions: existing international humanitarian law assigns responsibility to human commanders, but the complexity and opacity of AI systems can obscure causal chains.

The US Department of Defense adopted ethical AI principles in 2020, but implementation across hundreds of disparate programs is uneven. NATO's updated AI strategy includes accountability and transparency commitments, but like the US approach, these are principles rather than binding rules. China and Russia have not adopted equivalent public ethical frameworks for military AI.

Adversarial AI—the ability to fool or manipulate enemy AI systems—adds another layer of risk. As more weapons and sensors depend on AI for target classification and navigation, the potential for adversarial attacks to cause misidentification or fratricide grows.

### Industry & Innovation Ecosystem

Perhaps the most consequential structural change in defense AI is the shift from traditional prime contractors to Silicon Valley startups. The consortium led by Palantir and Anduril—including SpaceX, OpenAI, Scale AI, and Saronic—represents a new axis of defense-industrial power that explicitly challenges the Lockheed Martin–Northrop Grumman–RTX oligopoly.

Palantir's Maven Smart System, awarded a $480 million contract in 2024, provides AI-driven data integration and targeting to combatant commands. Anduril secured a $100 million CDAO award in December 2024 for edge data integration and reached a valuation of approximately $14 billion. OpenAI's partnership with Anduril—its first major military collaboration—marks a significant shift for a company that previously restricted military applications of its technology.

Venture capital is flowing aggressively into defense tech. Saronic (autonomous ships), Saildrone (uncrewed ocean surveillance), Red Cat (drones), and Shield AI (autonomous pilots for fighter jets) have all attracted substantial investment. These companies promise faster iteration, lower costs, and software-centric approaches that contrast with traditional hardware-heavy procurement.

Legacy contractors are not standing still. Lockheed Martin, RTX, and L3Harris are building partnerships, acquiring AI startups, and developing in-house AI capabilities. The competitive dynamic is driving faster innovation across the entire sector, but it also raises questions about intellectual property, security clearance, and the long-term stability of the defense-industrial base.

By May 2026, the Pentagon plans to use AI for financial audits—a mundane but significant application that demonstrates the technology's spread beyond combat systems. CDAO's development of custom AI "wingman" digital assistants for commanders points to a future in which AI is embedded in every level of military decision-making.

---

## Conclusion

The year 2026 marks a inflection point for AI in national security and defense. The technology is no longer a future prospect—it is embedded in active weapons systems, operational planning, and administrative processes across the world's major militaries. The United States retains a lead in both technological capability and institutional innovation, but China's systematic pursuit of intelligentized warfare, Russia's wartime adaptation, and NATO's collective investments mean that strategic competition will intensify rather than plateau.

The critical bottlenecks ahead are not primarily technological. Trusted data pipelines, resilience against adversarial AI, and—most urgently—an international legal framework for autonomous targeting remain unresolved. Without binding rules on meaningful human control, the risk of rapid, uncontrolled escalation driven by AI-enabled systems will grow. The convergence of military necessity, commercial momentum, and regulatory uncertainty makes AI in national security the defining defense policy challenge of the decade.
